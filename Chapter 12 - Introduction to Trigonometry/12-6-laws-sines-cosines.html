Trigonometry for every triangle

SOH-CAH-TOA works only in right triangles. Most triangles in the real world aren’t right triangles. Two laws extend trigonometry to every triangle, and they close out the course by tying together congruence, similarity, and the Pythagorean Theorem.

How oblique triangles are named

SIDE a IS OPPOSITE ∠A, AND SO ON

Lowercase letters are sides; each is across from the uppercase angle with the same letter.

An oblique triangle has no right angle. This naming system is used by both laws.

Law of Sines

For AAS and ASA

Law of Sines
a ÷ sin A = b ÷ sin B = c ÷ sin C

Problem: In △ABC, A = 40°, B = 60°, and a = 10. Find b.

1
Set up
10 ÷ sin 40° = b ÷ sin 60°
2
Solve
b = 10 sin 60° ÷ sin 40° ≈ 10(0.8660) ÷ 0.6428 ≈ 13.47

Law of Cosines

For SAS and SSS

Law of Cosines
c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C

Problem: In △ABC, a = 7, b = 8, and C = 60°. Find c.

1
Substitute
c² = 49 + 64 − 2(7)(8)(0.5) = 113 − 56 = 57
2
Solve
c = √57 ≈ 7.55

If C = 90°, then cos C = 0 and the law becomes c² = a² + b², the Pythagorean Theorem. The Law of Cosines is Pythagoras with a correction term for angles that aren’t 90°.

Worked Example

Finding an angle from three sides (SSS)

Problem: A triangle has sides a = 5, b = 7, and c = 8. Find ∠B, the angle opposite the side of 7.

1
Write the law for B
b² = a² + c² − 2ac cos B, so 49 = 25 + 64 − 80 cos B
2
Solve for cos B
80 cos B = 40, so cos B = 0.5
3
Find the angle
B = cos−1(0.5) = 60°

Which Law?

Match the law to the information

You know...Use
AAS or ASA (two angles, one side)Law of Sines
SAS (two sides, included angle)Law of Cosines
SSS (three sides)Law of Cosines
SSA (two sides, angle not between them)Law of Sines, but watch out

SSA is the ambiguous case. Just as SSA was not a congruence shortcut in Chapter 4, the given information can fit two different triangles, one triangle, or none at all.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Choose the law.

Question 1 — You know two sides and the angle between them. Which law finds the third side?

Here's why: SAS gives a, b, and C, exactly the pieces in c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C. The Law of Sines needs an angle opposite a known side.

Question 2 — You know two angles and a side opposite one of them. Which law should you use?

Here's why: AAS gives a matching side-angle pair, which is what the Law of Sines needs.

Why This Matters

Navigation, surveying, and astronomy

Ship navigators and surveyors use triangulation: measure the angles to a far-off point from both ends of a known baseline, and the Law of Sines gives the distances. This is how early surveyors mapped whole countries and measured the height of Mount Everest.

Real world: astronomers use the same idea, measuring a star’s position six months apart from opposite sides of Earth’s orbit, to find the distance to nearby stars.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
In △ABC, a = 5, b = 8, and C = 60°. Find c.
Here's why: c² = 25 + 64 − 2(5)(8)(0.5) = 89 − 40 = 49, so c = 7. √89 forgets the −2ab cos C term.
Question 2
When C = 90°, the Law of Cosines becomes...
Here's why: cos 90° = 0, so the last term disappears and c² = a² + b².
Question 3
In △ABC, A = 30°, B = 90°, and a = 6. Find b.
Here's why: 6 ÷ sin 30° = b ÷ sin 90°, so b = 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12. In a 30-60-90 triangle, the hypotenuse is twice the shorter leg, which confirms it.
Question 4
You know all three sides of a triangle. Which tool finds an angle?
Here's why: SSS gives no angle to start from, so the Law of Sines can’t begin. Solve the Law of Cosines for cos of the angle.
Question 5 — True or False
In the SSA case, the given information can sometimes fit two different triangles.
Here's why: True. That is why SSA is called the ambiguous case, and why it is not a congruence shortcut.
