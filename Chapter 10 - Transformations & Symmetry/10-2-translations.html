Let's Begin

Slide it, don’t flip it

A translation slides every point of a figure the same distance in the same direction. No flipping, no turning. It is the simplest transformation, and it turns out to be two reflections in disguise.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Describe a translation with a rule or a vector
  • 2Translate points and figures on the coordinate plane
  • 3Explain how two reflections over parallel lines make a translation

Vocabulary

Rules and vectors

A translation that moves every point a units horizontally and b units vertically has the rule (x, y) → (x + a, y + b). The same move can be written as the vector ⟨a, b⟩, an arrow showing direction and distance.

Positive a moves right, negative a moves left. Positive b moves up, negative b moves down. The image is congruent to the pre-image and keeps the same orientation.

Worked Example

Applying a rule

Problem: Translate △ABC with A(1, 1), B(3, 1), C(2, 4) by the vector ⟨4, −2⟩.

1
Write the rule
(x, y) → (x + 4, y − 2)
2
Apply it to each vertex
A′(5, −1), B′(7, −1), C′(6, 2)
3
Check
Each vertex moved 4 right and 2 down, matching the diagram.

Worked Example

Finding the rule from a point and its image

Problem: A translation takes A(2, 3) to A′(−1, 7). What is the rule?

1
Horizontal change
−1 − 2 = −3, so 3 units left
2
Vertical change
7 − 3 = 4, so 4 units up
3
Write it
(x, y) → (x − 3, y + 4), or the vector ⟨−3, 4⟩

A Surprising Connection

Two reflections make a translation

Reflect a figure over a line, then reflect the image over a second line parallel to the first. The final image is a translation of the original, perpendicular to the lines, by twice the distance between them.

Example: reflect over x = 1, then over x = 4. The lines are 3 units apart, so every point slides 6 units right. Try it with (0, 2): it goes to (2, 2), then to (6, 2).

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Use the rule or vector.

Question 1 — Translate (4, −1) with the rule (x, y) → (x − 5, y + 3).

Here's why: 4 − 5 = −1 and −1 + 3 = 2, so (−1, 2). (9, −4) moves in the opposite direction on both.

Question 2 — Which rule takes (0, 0) to (6, −2)?

Here's why: From (0, 0), the point moved 6 right and 2 down: add 6 to x and subtract 2 from y.

Why This Matters

Conveyor belts, chess, and computer animation

A rook moving along a row in chess is a translation. So is a box riding a conveyor belt or an elevator moving between floors: every point of the object moves the same distance in the same direction.

Real world: animators move characters across the screen frame by frame using translation vectors, and repeated wallpaper and fabric patterns are built by translating one tile over and over.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
Translate (3, 5) by the vector ⟨−2, 4⟩.
Here's why: 3 + (−2) = 1 and 5 + 4 = 9. (−6, 20) multiplies instead of adding.
Question 2
Which rule maps P(1, −2) to P′(5, 3)?
Here's why: 5 − 1 = 4 and 3 − (−2) = 5, so add 4 to x and 5 to y.
Question 3
Reflecting over two parallel lines that are 3 units apart produces...
Here's why: Two reflections over parallel lines make a translation of twice the distance between the lines: 2 × 3 = 6. Intersecting lines would make a rotation.
Question 4
What changes when a figure is translated?
Here's why: A translation only moves the figure. Size, shape, angles, and orientation all stay the same.
Question 5 — True or False
A translation keeps the orientation of a figure.
Here's why: True. Nothing is flipped, so a clockwise labeling stays clockwise. Reflections are the ones that reverse orientation.
