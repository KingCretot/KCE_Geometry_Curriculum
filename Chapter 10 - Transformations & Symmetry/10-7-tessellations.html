Covering the plane with no gaps

A tessellation is a pattern of shapes that covers a flat surface completely, with no gaps and no overlaps. Floor tiles, honeycombs, and brick walls are all tessellations, and every one of them is built from the transformations in this chapter.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Explain why only three regular polygons tessellate by themselves
  • 2Test whether a combination of regular polygons can meet at a vertex
  • 3Identify the transformations that build a tessellation

Core Idea

The angles at each vertex must add to 360°

Wherever corners of the tiles meet, their angles must fill a full turn exactly: 360°. Any less leaves a gap, and any more forces an overlap.

Regular Tessellations

Only three regular polygons work alone

REGULAR HEXAGONS: 3 MEET AT EVERY VERTEX

This is the pattern bees use in a honeycomb.

A regular tessellation uses one regular polygon. Only equilateral triangles, squares, and regular hexagons can do it, because only their angles divide 360° evenly. Polygons with more than six sides have angles bigger than 120°, so three won’t fit and two aren’t enough.

Semiregular Tessellations

Mixing regular polygons

OCTAGONS AND SQUARES: 135° + 135° + 90° = 360°

A semiregular tessellation uses two or more regular polygons, with the same arrangement at every vertex. There are exactly eight of them. The octagon-and-square pattern above is common on bathroom floors.

Worked Example

Testing a vertex

Problem: Can two regular 12-gons and one equilateral triangle meet at a vertex? Can regular octagons tessellate alone?

1
Interior angle of a regular 12-gon
(12 − 2) × 180° ÷ 12 = 150°
2
Add the angles
150° + 150° + 60° = 360°. Yes, they fit exactly.
3
Octagons alone
Each angle is 135°, and 360 ÷ 135 is not a whole number, so no. They need squares to fill the gaps.

Beyond Regular Polygons

Any triangle, any quadrilateral, and Escher

Every triangle tessellates: put six copies around a point, and the angles a, b, c each appear twice, for 2(a + b + c) = 360°. Every quadrilateral tessellates too, since its four angles already add to 360°.

Artist M. C. Escher made famous tessellations of birds, fish, and lizards. He started with a polygon that tessellates, cut a shape out of one side, and attached it to another side by a translation, rotation, or glide reflection, so the tiles still fit perfectly.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Check the angles at a vertex.

Question 1 — Each angle of a regular hexagon is 120°. How many hexagons meet at each vertex of a hexagon tessellation?

Here's why: 360° ÷ 120° = 3. Six would be the count for equilateral triangles.

Question 2 — Can regular pentagons tessellate the plane by themselves?

Here's why: Three pentagons fill 324° and leave a gap; four would need 432° and overlap.

Why This Matters

Floors, honeycombs, and soccer balls

Tile setters choose squares, hexagons, or octagon-and-square patterns because only patterns that tessellate cover a floor without cutting tiles into odd shapes. Honeybees build hexagons because that shape tessellates and uses the least wax for each cell.

Real world: a classic soccer ball is hexagons and pentagons, but that is a pattern on a sphere, not a flat tessellation. The curve of the ball is what lets the pentagons fit.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
How many regular polygons can tessellate the plane by themselves?
Here's why: Only the equilateral triangle, square, and regular hexagon have angles that divide 360° evenly.
Question 2
Which regular polygon CANNOT tessellate by itself?
Here's why: A pentagon’s 108° angle does not divide 360° evenly. The other three do.
Question 3
How many squares meet at each vertex of a square tessellation?
Here's why: 90° × 4 = 360°.
Question 4
A semiregular tessellation uses two regular octagons and one ____ at each vertex.
Here's why: 135° + 135° = 270°, and 360° − 270° = 90°, the angle of a square.
Question 5 — True or False
Every quadrilateral, even an irregular one, tessellates the plane.
Here's why: True. Its four angles add to 360°, so four copies, one of each corner, fit around every vertex.
