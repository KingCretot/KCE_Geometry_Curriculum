Key Ideas · Lessons 10.1–10.3

The three basic moves

Lesson 10.1

Reflections

The line of reflection is the perpendicular bisector of each point-to-image segment. Over the x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y). Over the y-axis: (x, y) → (−x, y). Over y = x: (x, y) → (y, x). Reflections reverse orientation.

Example: Reflect (−3, 4) over the x-axis: (−3, −4).
Reflection over the y-axis
Lesson 10.2

Translations

Every point slides the same distance and direction: (x, y) → (x + a, y + b), or by the vector ⟨a, b⟩. Two reflections over parallel lines make a translation of twice the distance between the lines.

Example: (3, 5) by ⟨−2, 4⟩ → (1, 9).
Lesson 10.3

Rotations about the origin

90° counterclockwise: (x, y) → (−y, x). 180°: (x, y) → (−x, −y). 270° counterclockwise (= 90° clockwise): (x, y) → (y, −x). Two reflections over lines meeting at angle θ make a rotation of 2θ.

Example: Rotate (3, 1) by 90° counterclockwise: (−1, 3).
90° counterclockwise about the origin

Key Ideas · Lessons 10.4–10.5

Isometries and symmetry

Lesson 10.4

Isometries keep size and shape

Reflections, translations, rotations, and glide reflections all preserve distance and angle measure. Translations and rotations are direct (orientation kept); reflections and glide reflections are opposite (orientation reversed).

Example: A glide reflection = translation + reflection over a line parallel to it.
Lesson 10.5

Symmetry

Line symmetry: a reflection maps the figure onto itself. Rotational symmetry: a rotation of less than 360° does; for a regular n-gon the smallest angle is 360°/n. Point symmetry is 180° rotational symmetry.

Example: A regular octagon has 8 lines of symmetry and turns onto itself every 45°.

Key Ideas · Lessons 10.6–10.7

Dilations and tessellations

Lesson 10.6

Dilations change size, not shape

Centered at the origin: (x, y) → (kx, ky). k > 1 enlarges, 0 < k < 1 reduces. Angles stay the same and lengths multiply by k, so a dilation is not an isometry (unless k = 1).

Example: Scale factor 2.5 turns a 12-unit side into 30.
Lesson 10.7

Tessellations

A tessellation covers the plane with no gaps or overlaps; the angles at each vertex sum to 360°. Only equilateral triangles, squares, and regular hexagons tessellate alone. Every triangle and every quadrilateral tessellates.

Example: Hexagons: 3 × 120° = 360°. Pentagons fail: 108° doesn't divide 360°.

Watch Out

The mistakes that cost the most points

The mistakeThe fix
Changing the wrong coordinate in a reflectionOver the x-axis, y changes sign. Over the y-axis, x changes sign.
Mixing up clockwise and counterclockwiseCounterclockwise is the positive direction. 90° CW is the same as 270° CCW: (x, y) → (y, −x).
Two reflections = rotation by θThe rotation is twice the angle between the lines (and translation is twice the distance).
Calling a dilation an isometryDilations change lengths, so they aren't isometries.
Reading a translation rule backwardImage minus pre-image gives the shift: (2, −1) → (−3, 4) is (x − 5, y + 5).
Assuming any regular polygon tessellatesOnly if its interior angle divides 360°: triangle 60°, square 90°, hexagon 120°.

Practice Test

10 questions, every lesson mixed together

Just like a unit test, these jump between lessons. You get two tries on each question, and every question explains itself after you answer.

Question 1
Reflect (4, −2) over the y-axis.
Here's why: Over the y-axis, the x-coordinate changes sign and y stays: (−4, −2). (4, 2) is the reflection over the x-axis.
Question 2
Reflect (3, −5) over the line y = x.
Here's why: Reflecting over y = x swaps the coordinates: (3, −5) → (−5, 3).
Question 3
Which rule maps A(2, −1) to A′(−3, 4)?
Here's why: x goes from 2 to −3 (down 5) and y goes from −1 to 4 (up 5). The second choice runs the move backward.
Question 4
Rotate (2, 5) by 90° counterclockwise about the origin.
Here's why: 90° counterclockwise: (x, y) → (−y, x) = (−5, 2). (5, −2) is the clockwise rotation, and (−2, −5) is 180°.
Question 5
Rotate (−4, 1) by 270° counterclockwise about the origin.
Here's why: 270° counterclockwise equals 90° clockwise: (x, y) → (y, −x) = (1, −(−4)) = (1, 4). (−1, −4) is the 90° counterclockwise rotation.
Question 6
A figure is reflected over two lines that intersect at a 35° angle. What single transformation is the result?
Here's why: Two reflections over intersecting lines make a rotation about their intersection point by twice the angle between them: 2 × 35° = 70°.
Question 7
Which transformation reverses a figure's orientation?
Here's why: Reflections (and glide reflections) are opposite isometries — they flip clockwise into counterclockwise. Translations and rotations keep orientation.
Question 8
What is the smallest rotation that maps a regular pentagon onto itself?
Here's why: A regular n-gon has rotational symmetry every 360°/n: 360° ÷ 5 = 72°. 108° is the pentagon's interior angle.
Question 9
Dilate (8, −6) about the origin by a scale factor of ½.
Here's why: Multiply each coordinate by ½: (4, −3). (16, −12) multiplies by 2, and (8.5, −5.5) adds ½ instead of multiplying.
Question 10
Which regular polygon CAN tessellate the plane by itself?
Here's why: A hexagon's 120° angles fit three at a vertex: 3 × 120° = 360°. A pentagon's 108°, an octagon's 135°, and a decagon's 144° don't divide 360°.
