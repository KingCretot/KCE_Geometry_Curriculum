Let's Begin

Combining moves that keep every distance

An isometry (“equal measure”) is a transformation that keeps every distance the same. Reflections, translations, and rotations are all isometries. Today we combine them, meet a fourth isometry, and see that the order of the moves matters.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Define isometry and name the four types
  • 2Perform a composition of two transformations
  • 3Tell direct isometries from opposite isometries

Vocabulary

The four isometries

IsometryOrientation
ReflectionOpposite (reverses)
TranslationDirect (keeps)
RotationDirect (keeps)
Glide reflectionOpposite (reverses)

A glide reflection is a translation followed by a reflection over a line parallel to the direction of the translation. Footprints in sand are the classic example: each step is the previous one, slid forward and flipped.

A direct isometry keeps orientation; an opposite isometry reverses it. Every isometry is congruence in action: the image is always congruent to the pre-image.

Composition

Doing one transformation after another

A composition applies one transformation, then applies a second one to the image. Order matters, so always work from the first move to the second.

1
Reflect over the x-axis, then rotate 90°
(2, 1) → (2, −1) → (1, 2)
2
Rotate 90°, then reflect over the x-axis
(2, 1) → (−1, 2) → (−1, −2)

Same two moves, different order, different result. Every rotation here is counterclockwise about the origin.

Worked Example

A glide reflection with coordinates

Problem: Translate (2, 1) by ⟨3, 0⟩, then reflect the result over the x-axis.

1
Translate
(2 + 3, 1) = (5, 1)
2
Reflect over the x-axis
(5, −1)
3
Name it
The translation is horizontal and the reflection line (the x-axis) is horizontal, so they are parallel. This is a glide reflection.

Big Idea

Every isometry is made of reflections

Any isometry of the plane can be built from at most three reflections. One reflection is a reflection. Two over parallel lines make a translation. Two over intersecting lines make a rotation. Three can make a glide reflection.

That is why the reflection is the building block of this whole chapter.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Think about compositions.

Question 1 — A trail of footprints in the sand shows which isometry?

Here's why: Each print is the one before it slid forward and flipped left-to-right: a translation plus a parallel reflection. A dilation changes size, so it isn’t an isometry at all.

Question 2 — Reflect (1, 3) over the y-axis, then translate by ⟨0, −4⟩. Where does it land?

Here's why: Reflect: (−1, 3). Then move down 4: (−1, −1). (1, −1) skips the reflection.

Why This Matters

Robots, quilts, and video games

A robot arm in a factory reaches a part by composing moves: rotate the base, slide the arm, turn the wrist. Programmers describe each step as a transformation and combine them in order.

Real world: quilt makers and tile designers repeat one block across a pattern by translating, rotating, and flipping it, all isometries, so every copy is exactly the same size.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
An isometry always keeps...
Here's why: Isometry means equal measure: every length and angle is unchanged. Orientation can flip, and position usually changes.
Question 2
Which of these is NOT an isometry?
Here's why: A dilation with scale factor 3 triples every length, so distances change. The other three keep every distance.
Question 3
A glide reflection combines...
Here's why: Slide, then flip over a line parallel to the slide.
Question 4
Reflect (3, 2) over the x-axis, then translate by ⟨−5, 0⟩. Where does it land?
Here's why: Reflect: (3, −2). Then 5 left: (−2, −2). (−2, 2) skips the reflection.
Question 5 — True or False
A reflection is a direct isometry.
Here's why: False. A reflection reverses orientation, so it is an opposite isometry. Translations and rotations are direct.
