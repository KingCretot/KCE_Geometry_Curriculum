Let's Begin

When a figure lands exactly on itself

A figure has symmetry when some transformation maps it exactly onto itself. Butterflies, snowflakes, stop signs, and many letters of the alphabet all have it. Today we name the kinds and count them.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify line symmetry and count lines of symmetry
  • 2Find the order and angle of rotational symmetry
  • 3Recognize point symmetry

Kind 1

Line (reflectional) symmetry

A figure has line symmetry if a reflection over some line maps it onto itself. That line is a line of symmetry: fold along it and the two halves match exactly.

A regular polygon with n sides has exactly n lines of symmetry. A rectangle that isn’t a square has only 2, through the midpoints of opposite sides, not along the diagonals.

Kind 2

Rotational symmetry

A figure has rotational symmetry if a rotation of less than 360° about its center maps it onto itself. The order is how many times it lands on itself in one full turn, and the smallest such angle is 360° ÷ order.

A REGULAR HEXAGON: 6 LINES, ORDER 6

Each dashed line is a line of symmetry. A turn of 360° ÷ 6 = 60° also maps the hexagon onto itself.

Kind 3

Point symmetry

A figure has point symmetry if a 180° rotation about its center maps it onto itself. Every point has a matching point the same distance away on the opposite side of the center.

Worked Example

Counting symmetries

Problem: Describe all the symmetry of (a) a regular hexagon and (b) an equilateral triangle.

1
Hexagon
6 lines of symmetry; rotational symmetry of order 6 with smallest angle 60°; point symmetry, since 180° is a multiple of 60°.
2
Equilateral triangle
3 lines of symmetry; rotational symmetry of order 3 with smallest angle 120°; no point symmetry, since 180° is not a multiple of 120°.

In 3-D

Symmetry in solids

Solids can have plane symmetry, where a plane slices the solid into two mirror-image halves, and axis symmetry, where turning the solid around a line maps it onto itself.

A cylinder has infinitely many planes of symmetry through its axis, plus one plane halfway up. A cube has 9 planes of symmetry.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Count the symmetry.

Question 1 — How many lines of symmetry does a square have?

Here's why: A square has 2 lines through the midpoints of opposite sides and 2 along its diagonals: 4 in all, matching n = 4 for a regular polygon.

Question 2 — What is the smallest rotation that maps a regular pentagon onto itself?

Here's why: 360° ÷ 5 = 72°. 108° is the pentagon’s interior angle, which is a different measurement.

Why This Matters

Nature builds with symmetry

Snowflakes almost always have six-fold rotational symmetry because of how water molecules link as they freeze. Starfish have five-fold symmetry, and most animals, including people, have one line of symmetry down the middle.

Real world: car designers make wheels and hubcaps with rotational symmetry so they look balanced while spinning, and engineers rely on symmetry to balance weight in airplanes and bridges.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
How many lines of symmetry does a regular octagon have?
Here's why: A regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry: 8.
Question 2
What is the smallest rotation that maps a regular decagon (10 sides) onto itself?
Here's why: 360° ÷ 10 = 36°.
Question 3
Which letter has point symmetry but no line symmetry?
Here's why: Z looks the same after a 180° turn, but no fold line matches its halves. A, M, and T each have a vertical line of symmetry and no point symmetry.
Question 4
How many lines of symmetry does a rectangle that is not a square have?
Here's why: Only the two lines through the midpoints of opposite sides. The diagonals do not fold a non-square rectangle onto itself.
Question 5 — True or False
A figure with point symmetry has 180° rotational symmetry.
Here's why: True. That is exactly the definition of point symmetry.
