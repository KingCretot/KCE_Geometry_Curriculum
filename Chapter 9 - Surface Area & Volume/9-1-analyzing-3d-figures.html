Let's Begin — Welcome to Chapter 9!

From flat shapes to solid ones you can hold

Chapters 7 and 8 measured flat shapes. Chapter 9 moves into three dimensions: boxes, cans, cones, and balls. Before we measure anything, we need to describe solids precisely: count their parts, slice them open, and see how they can be built by spinning a flat shape.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Use Euler's Formula to check a polyhedron's faces, edges, and vertices
  • 2Sketch and name the cross section made by slicing a solid with a plane
  • 3Identify the solid made by rotating a flat shape around a line

Vocabulary

Two families of solids

A polyhedron is a solid whose surfaces are all flat polygons, called faces. Faces meet at edges, and edges meet at vertices. Prisms and pyramids are polyhedra.

Solids with a curved surface are not polyhedra: a cylinder (two circle bases), a cone (one circle base and an apex), and a sphere (every point the same distance from the center).

A regular polyhedron has faces that are all the same regular polygon, with the same number of faces meeting at every vertex. There are exactly five of them: the tetrahedron (4 faces), cube (6), octahedron (8), dodecahedron (12), and icosahedron (20). Dice in tabletop games use all five.

Cross Sections

Slice a solid with a plane

A cross section is the flat shape you see when a plane slices through a solid. The same solid can give different cross sections depending on the angle of the cut.

SolidCut parallel to the baseCut perpendicular to the baseOther cuts
CubeSquareRectangle (cut straight down through two opposite edges)Triangle (cut off a corner)
CylinderCircleRectangle (cut straight down through the axis)Oval (a tilted cut)
ConeCircleTriangle (cut straight down through the apex)Oval (a tilted cut)

Surprise result: slicing a cube at just the right tilt can even give a regular hexagon.

Solids of Revolution

Spin a flat shape and a solid appears

Rotate a flat shape completely around a line (the axis), and it sweeps out a solid of revolution.

A pottery wheel works exactly this way: every bowl and vase thrown on a wheel is a solid of revolution.

Worked Example

Using Euler's Formula to find a missing count

Problem: A hexagonal prism has 2 hexagon bases and 6 rectangular sides. It has 12 vertices. How many edges does it have?

1
Count the faces
2 bases + 6 sides = 8 faces
2
Substitute into V − E + F = 2
12 − E + 8 = 2
3
Solve
20 − E = 2, so E = 18
4
Check by counting
6 edges on the top hexagon, 6 on the bottom, and 6 vertical edges joining them: 18. It checks out.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

An octagonal prism has 10 faces and 16 vertices.

Question 1 — How many edges does it have?

Here's why: Use V − E + F = 2: 16 − E + 10 = 2, so 26 − E = 2 and E = 24. Counting confirms it: 8 edges on top, 8 on the bottom, 8 vertical. 26 is V + F, not the answer to the equation.

Question 2 — A cylinder is sliced by a plane parallel to its bases. What is the cross section?

Here's why: A slice parallel to the bases is a copy of a base, and a cylinder’s bases are circles. A rectangle comes from cutting straight down through the axis.

Why This Matters

Doctors, engineers, and 3-D printers all think in slices

A CT scan or MRI builds a picture of the body as a stack of cross sections, one thin slice at a time. A 3-D printer does the reverse: it builds an object layer by layer, and every layer is a cross section of the finished shape.

Real world: a machinist turning a part on a lathe is making a solid of revolution. The spinning metal and the cutting tool together trace out a cylinder, cone, or curved profile.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
A pentagonal pyramid has 6 vertices and 6 faces. How many edges does it have?
Here's why: 6 − E + 6 = 2 gives E = 10. Count to check: 5 edges around the base and 5 edges climbing to the apex.
Question 2
Which of these is NOT a polyhedron?
Here's why: A cone has a curved surface and a circle base, so it is not made of polygons. The other three have only flat polygon faces.
Question 3
Rotating a right triangle completely around one of its legs makes a...
Here's why: The leg on the axis becomes the height, the other leg sweeps out a circle base, and the hypotenuse sweeps out the slanted side: a cone. A rectangle would make a cylinder.
Question 4
Slicing a cube with a plane parallel to one of its faces gives a...
Here's why: A slice parallel to a face is a copy of that face, and every face of a cube is a square. A triangle comes from cutting off a corner.
Question 5 — True or False
There are exactly five regular polyhedra.
Here's why: True. The tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron are the only five, a fact the ancient Greeks proved.
