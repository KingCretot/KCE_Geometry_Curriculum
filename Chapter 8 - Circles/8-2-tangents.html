Let's Begin

Lines that touch a circle at exactly one point

A tangent line touches a circle at precisely one point, called the point of tangency, and never crosses into the circle's interior. Today we look at the special relationship every tangent has with the radius drawn to its point of tangency, plus what happens when two tangents meet outside a circle.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify a tangent line and its point of tangency
  • 2Apply the Tangent-Radius Perpendicularity Theorem
  • 3Apply the Two-Tangent Theorem

Core Theorem

Tangent-Radius Perpendicularity Theorem

THE RADIUS IS ALWAYS PERPENDICULAR TO THE TANGENT

A line is tangent to a circle if and only if it is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency. This is a biconditional — both directions are true, and it's the single fact that unlocks nearly every tangent problem.

Core Theorem

The Two-Tangent Theorem

If two tangent segments are drawn to a circle from the same external point, those two segments are congruent. The external point, the center, and the two points of tangency form a kite — which explains why the two tangent lengths always match.

Worked Example

Finding a tangent segment's length

Problem: A tangent segment is drawn from external point P to a circle with radius 9. The distance from P to the center is 15. Find the tangent segment's length.

1
Set up the right triangle
The radius is perpendicular to the tangent at the point of tangency, so radius, tangent segment, and the segment from P to center form a right triangle.
2
Apply the Pythagorean Theorem
9² + t² = 15² → 81 + t² = 225 → t² = 144
3
Solve
t = √144 = 12

Worked Example

Applying the Two-Tangent Theorem

Problem: Two tangent segments from the same external point are labeled 3x + 2 and 5x − 6. Find x.

1
Set the segments equal
By the Two-Tangent Theorem: 3x + 2 = 5x − 6
2
Solve for x
8 = 2x → x = 4

Why This Matters

Belts, gears, and the horizon

A belt looped around two circular pulleys touches each pulley along a tangent line — mechanical engineers use the Tangent-Radius Perpendicularity Theorem to calculate exactly how much belt length is needed.

Real world: the line of sight to the horizon from a height above the Earth is tangent to the Earth's circular cross-section — this exact geometry is used to calculate how far you can see from a given altitude.
