Let's Begin

Building circle-related figures with only a compass and straightedge

Compass-and-straightedge construction goes back to Chapter 3's parallel-line constructions. Today we apply the same discipline to circles: constructing a tangent line to a circle from an external point, and constructing the circle that passes through (or is inscribed in) a triangle.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Construct a tangent line to a circle from an external point
  • 2Construct a circumscribed circle around a triangle
  • 3Construct an inscribed circle within a triangle

Core Construction

Constructing a tangent from an external point

Using the Tangent-Radius Perpendicularity Theorem from Lesson 8.2: find the midpoint of the segment from the external point to the center, draw a circle centered there through both points, and its two intersections with the original circle are the two points of tangency.

Core Construction

Constructing a circumscribed circle

Recall from Lesson 5.1 that a triangle's circumcenter (where all three perpendicular bisectors meet) is equidistant from all three vertices. Construct all three perpendicular bisectors; their intersection is the center of the circumscribed circle, and the distance to any vertex is the radius.

Core Construction

Constructing an inscribed circle

Recall from Lesson 5.2 that a triangle's incenter (where all three angle bisectors meet) is equidistant from all three sides. Construct all three angle bisectors; their intersection is the center of the inscribed circle, and the perpendicular distance to any side is the radius.

Core Concept

Why these constructions rely on Chapter 5

This lesson doesn't introduce new theorems — it applies circumcenter and incenter facts from Chapter 5 to a new purpose: actually drawing the circle, not just locating a point. The circumscribed circle passes through all three vertices; the inscribed circle touches all three sides.

Why This Matters

Manhole covers and coin design

Manufacturers designing a circular part that must fit tightly around a triangular bracket use inscribed-circle construction to determine the exact maximum circle diameter that fits inside.

Real world: the circumscribed circle construction is how engineers find the smallest circular material blank that can contain a triangular part, minimizing wasted material.
