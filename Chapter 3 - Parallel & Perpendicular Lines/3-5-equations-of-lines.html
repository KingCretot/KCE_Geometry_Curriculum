From a slope and a point, to a full equation

Every idea from this chapter — parallel angle relationships, constructions, distance, midpoint, slope — lands here. Today you'll write the actual equation of a line, and use everything you've learned to write equations for lines parallel or perpendicular to a given one.

Form 1

Slope-intercept form

Slope-Intercept Form
y = mx + b

m is the slope from Lesson 3.4. b is the y-intercept — the point where the line crosses the y-axis, at (0, b). This form is easiest to use when you already know both the slope and where the line crosses the y-axis.

Form 2

Point-slope form

Point-Slope Form
y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)

(x₁, y₁) is any known point on the line, and m is the slope. Use this form whenever you're given a slope and a single point that isn't the y-intercept — then simplify into slope-intercept form if needed.

Worked Example

Writing an equation from a point and a slope

Problem: Write the equation of the line through (2, 5) with slope 3.

1
Start with point-slope form
y − 5 = 3(x − 2)
2
Distribute
y − 5 = 3x − 6
3
Solve for y — slope-intercept form
y = 3x − 1

Worked Example

Writing a parallel line's equation

Problem: Write the equation of the line parallel to y = 2x + 1 that passes through (3, 4).

1
Parallel lines share a slope
The new line must also have slope m = 2.
2
Use point-slope form
y − 4 = 2(x − 3)
3
Simplify
y − 4 = 2x − 6  →  y = 2x − 2

Why This Matters

Every straight design decision, written as one equation

An architect designing a roofline that must stay parallel to an existing roof, or a road engineer laying out a new street that must intersect an existing one at a true right angle, writes exactly these equations before a single beam is cut or a single foot of asphalt is poured.

Real world: economists use slope-intercept form constantly — a cost equation like C = 15x + 200 (m = 15, b = 200) says exactly what you learned today: 200 is a fixed starting cost, and 15 is the rate the cost climbs for each additional unit.
