Let's Begin

Two more special points inside every triangle

Last lesson, perpendicular bisectors and altitudes gave us the circumcenter and orthocenter. Today, two new special segments — angle bisectors and medians — give us the incenter and the centroid.

  • 1Construct angle bisectors of a triangle and identify the incenter
  • 2Explain why the incenter is equidistant from all three sides

Building Block 1

Angle bisectors → the incenter

1
Definition
An angle bisector of a triangle is a ray that splits one of its interior angles into two equal parts. A triangle has three angles, so it has three angle bisectors.
2
The Concurrency
All three angle bisectors meet at one point, called the incenter.
3
Why it matters
Every point on an angle bisector is equidistant from the two sides that form that angle. Since the incenter sits on all three bisectors, it is equidistant from all three sides — that shared distance is the radius of the largest circle that fits perfectly inside the triangle (the inscribed circle).

See It

The incenter and its inscribed circle

INCENTER: EQUIDISTANT FROM ALL THREE SIDES

Key fact: unlike the circumcenter, the incenter is always inside the triangle — no matter whether the triangle is acute, right, or obtuse.

Building Block 2

Medians → the centroid

1
Definition
A median of a triangle connects a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Every triangle has three medians.
2
The Concurrency
All three medians meet at one point, called the centroid — the triangle's center of balance (center of mass).
3
Centroid Theorem
The centroid divides every median into a 2:1 ratio, measured from the vertex to the centroid to the midpoint. The piece from vertex to centroid is always twice as long as the piece from centroid to midpoint.

Worked Example

Using the 2:1 ratio

Problem: Median AM has length 18. Point G is the centroid. Find AG and GM.

1
Set up the ratio
AG : GM = 2 : 1, and AG + GM = 18
2
Split into 3 equal parts
18 ÷ 3 = 6, so 1 part = 6
3
Conclusion
AG = 2 parts = 12, and GM = 1 part = 6   (check: 12 + 6 = 18 ✓)

Why This Matters

Balance and inscribed circles in the real world

The centroid is exactly where a physical triangular object — a metal plate, a sail, a drone wing — will balance perfectly on a single pin. Engineers calculate centroids constantly when designing anything that needs to stay stable.

Real world: the incenter and inscribed circle idea is exactly how architects fit the largest possible circular window, table, or fountain inside a triangular room or plot of land.
