Core Theorem

The Exterior Angle Inequality Theorem

The measure of an exterior angle of a triangle is always greater than the measure of either remote interior angle (the two interior angles that are not adjacent to it).

EXTERIOR ANGLE > EITHER REMOTE INTERIOR ANGLE

Recall from Lesson 4.1 that the Triangle Exterior Angle Theorem says the exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles. Since both remote interior angles are positive, the exterior angle must be larger than each one individually — this inequality theorem is really just a direct consequence of that equation.

Core Theorem

The Hinge Theorem (SAS Inequality)

Picture two triangles sharing two pairs of congruent sides, like a door hinge that can swing open wider or narrower. The Hinge Theorem tells you what happens to the third side as that "hinge angle" changes.

If two sides of one triangle are congruent to two sides of another triangle, but the included angle of the first is larger than the included angle of the second, then the third side of the first triangle is longer than the third side of the second.

SAME TWO SIDES, DIFFERENT ANGLE, DIFFERENT THIRD SIDE

Both triangles above have the same two side lengths marked — but the triangle with the wider included angle (right) has a longer third side. This matches everyday intuition: opening a hinge wider pushes the far ends farther apart.

Worked Example

Applying the Hinge Theorem

Problem: In ΔABC and ΔDEF, AB = DE and BC = EF. m∠B = 70° and m∠E = 95°. Which third side is longer, AC or DF?

1
Compare the included angles
∠B (70°) is the included angle in ΔABC; ∠E (95°) is the included angle in ΔDEF. 95° > 70°.
2
Apply the Hinge Theorem
The triangle with the larger included angle has the longer third side.
3
Conclusion
DF > AC

Why This Matters

Hinges, cranes, and adjustable structures

The Hinge Theorem is literally named for how hinges work — and it's exactly the math engineers use when designing anything with an adjustable angle: folding ramps, crane arms, robotic elbows, or adjustable easels. Knowing how the reach (the "third side") changes as the angle opens or closes is essential to safe design.

Real world: the Converse of the Hinge Theorem works in reverse — if you can only measure the reach (third side) of a mechanism, you can determine which of two settings has the wider angle without measuring the angle directly.
