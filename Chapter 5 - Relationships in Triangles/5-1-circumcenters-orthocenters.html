Every triangle hides four special points

Core Concept

Points of concurrency

When three or more lines all pass through the same single point, we call that a point of concurrency. Every triangle has several of these hidden inside it, formed by different special segments. Today's two both come from lines related to the triangle's sides and angles — one from perpendicular bisectors, one from altitudes.

THREE LINES, ONE MEETING POINT

Building Block 1

Perpendicular bisectors → the circumcenter

1
Definition
A perpendicular bisector of a segment cuts it in half at a 90° angle. A triangle has three sides, so it has three perpendicular bisectors — one per side.
2
The Concurrency
All three perpendicular bisectors of a triangle's sides meet at one point, called the circumcenter.
3
Why it matters
Every point on a perpendicular bisector of a segment is equidistant from that segment's two endpoints. Since the circumcenter sits on all three bisectors, it is equidistant from all three vertices — that shared distance is the radius of a circle that passes through all three corners of the triangle (the circumscribed circle, which is where "circumcenter" gets its name).

See It

The circumcenter and its circle

CIRCUMCENTER: EQUIDISTANT FROM A, B, C

Good to know: the circumcenter can land inside an acute triangle, exactly on the hypotenuse of a right triangle (its midpoint!), or outside an obtuse triangle entirely. Where it lands depends on the triangle's shape.

Building Block 2

Altitudes → the orthocenter

1
Definition
An altitude of a triangle is a perpendicular segment from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side. Every triangle has three altitudes, one from each vertex.
2
The Concurrency
All three altitudes meet at one point, called the orthocenter.
3
Don't confuse them
Perpendicular bisectors cut sides in half. Altitudes drop straight down from a vertex. Different starting points, different lines — that's why circumcenter and orthocenter are usually two different locations in the same triangle.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Consider a right triangle with the right angle at vertex C.

Question 1 — Where does the orthocenter of a right triangle always sit?

Here's why: The orthocenter is where the three altitudes meet. In a right triangle, each leg is already an altitude to the other leg, and the two legs meet at the right-angle vertex. So the orthocenter sits exactly on that corner.

Question 2 — The circumcenter of that same right triangle sits at the midpoint of which side?

Here's why: In a right triangle the circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse. That point is the same distance from all three vertices, so a circle centered there passes through every corner. The midpoint of a leg doesn't have that property.

Why This Matters

One circle, three points, real engineering

City planners use the circumcenter idea constantly: if you know the locations of three towns and want to build a single facility (a hospital, a cell tower) exactly the same distance from all three, the circumcenter of the triangle they form is the mathematically perfect spot.

Real world: the orthocenter shows up in structural engineering and truss design, where perpendicular load-bearing lines from each joint of a triangular support need to be tracked precisely to keep a structure balanced.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
Where three or more lines all meet at a single point, that point is called a...
Here's why: A point of concurrency is where three or more lines all meet at one point, like several roads meeting at a single roundabout. A vertex is just a corner of a figure.
Question 2
The circumcenter is formed by the intersection of a triangle's three...
Here's why: The circumcenter is where the three perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet. The point where the altitudes meet is the orthocenter, a different point.
Question 3
The circumcenter is always equidistant from...
Here's why: Every point on a perpendicular bisector is equally far from the two endpoints of that side. The circumcenter is on all three bisectors, so it's equally far from all three vertices. That's why it's the center of the circle drawn through the corners.
Question 4
An altitude of a triangle is a perpendicular segment from a...
Here's why: An altitude is a perpendicular segment from a vertex down to the line containing the opposite side, the way you'd measure a person's height straight down to the floor. A segment from one midpoint to another is a midsegment.
Question 5 — True or False
In an obtuse triangle, the circumcenter can lie outside the triangle.
Here's why: True. In an obtuse triangle the circumcenter lands outside the triangle. The wide angle pushes the perpendicular bisectors so that they meet beyond the long side.
