Core Concept

Points of concurrency

When three or more lines all pass through the same single point, we call that a point of concurrency. Every triangle has several of these hidden inside it, formed by different special segments. Today's two both come from lines related to the triangle's sides and angles — one from perpendicular bisectors, one from altitudes.

THREE LINES, ONE MEETING POINT

Building Block 1

Perpendicular bisectors → the circumcenter

1
Definition
A perpendicular bisector of a segment cuts it in half at a 90° angle. A triangle has three sides, so it has three perpendicular bisectors — one per side.
2
The Concurrency
All three perpendicular bisectors of a triangle's sides meet at one point, called the circumcenter.
3
Why it matters
Every point on a perpendicular bisector of a segment is equidistant from that segment's two endpoints. Since the circumcenter sits on all three bisectors, it is equidistant from all three vertices — that shared distance is the radius of a circle that passes through all three corners of the triangle (the circumscribed circle, which is where "circumcenter" gets its name).

See It

The circumcenter and its circle

CIRCUMCENTER: EQUIDISTANT FROM A, B, C

Good to know: the circumcenter can land inside an acute triangle, exactly on the hypotenuse of a right triangle (its midpoint!), or outside an obtuse triangle entirely. Where it lands depends on the triangle's shape.

Building Block 2

Altitudes → the orthocenter

1
Definition
An altitude of a triangle is a perpendicular segment from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side. Every triangle has three altitudes, one from each vertex.
2
The Concurrency
All three altitudes meet at one point, called the orthocenter.
3
Don't confuse them
Perpendicular bisectors cut sides in half. Altitudes drop straight down from a vertex. Different starting points, different lines — that's why circumcenter and orthocenter are usually two different locations in the same triangle.

Why This Matters

One circle, three points, real engineering

City planners use the circumcenter idea constantly: if you know the locations of three towns and want to build a single facility (a hospital, a cell tower) exactly the same distance from all three, the circumcenter of the triangle they form is the mathematically perfect spot.

Real world: the orthocenter shows up in structural engineering and truss design, where perpendicular load-bearing lines from each joint of a triangular support need to be tracked precisely to keep a structure balanced.
