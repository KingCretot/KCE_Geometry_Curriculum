Key Ideas · Lessons 5.1–5.2

Four points of concurrency

Lesson 5.1

Circumcenter and orthocenter

The perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet at the circumcenter, which is equidistant from the three vertices. The altitudes meet at the orthocenter. Both can fall outside an obtuse triangle.

Example: The circumcenter is the center of the circle through all three vertices.
Lesson 5.2

Incenter and centroid

The angle bisectors meet at the incenter, equidistant from the three sides and always inside. The medians meet at the centroid, which sits two-thirds of the way from each vertex to the opposite midpoint (a 2 : 1 split).

Example: A median of 30: vertex to centroid is 20, centroid to midpoint is 10.
Medians meet at the centroid G

Key Ideas · Lesson 5.3

Which triangles are possible

Lesson 5.3

Triangle Inequality and side–angle order

Any two sides must add to more than the third. For two known sides a and b, the third side x satisfies |a − b| < x < a + b. The longest side is opposite the largest angle, and the shortest side opposite the smallest angle.

Example: Sides 10 and 12: 2 < x < 22.

Key Ideas · Lessons 5.4–5.5

Contradiction and comparison

Lesson 5.4

Indirect proof

Assume the opposite of what you want to prove, reason until you hit a contradiction with a given fact, definition, or theorem, then conclude the assumption was false.

Example: To prove x ≠ 5, start with "Assume x = 5."
Lesson 5.5

Exterior Angle Inequality and Hinge Theorem

An exterior angle is greater than either remote interior angle. Hinge Theorem: two triangles with two pairs of congruent sides — the larger included angle gives the longer third side. The converse compares angles from third sides.

Example: Sides 5 and 7 with a 65° angle make a longer third side than 5 and 7 with a 40° angle.

Watch Out

The mistakes that cost the most points

The mistakeThe fix
Mixing up which point matches which segmentCircumcenter ↔ perpendicular bisectors. Incenter ↔ angle bisectors. Centroid ↔ medians. Orthocenter ↔ altitudes.
Mixing up vertices and sidesCircumcenter: equidistant from vertices. Incenter: equidistant from sides.
Using a 1 : 2 centroid split from the wrong endThe longer piece is always on the vertex side: vertex-to-centroid is twice centroid-to-midpoint.
Checking only one pair in the Triangle InequalityCheck that the two shortest sides add to more than the longest. Equal isn't enough: 4, 7, 11 fails.
Using ≤ for the third-side rangeThe range is strict: |a − b| < x < a + b.
Starting an indirect proof with the statement itselfStep one is always to assume the opposite.

Practice Test

10 questions, every lesson mixed together

Just like a unit test, these jump between lessons. You get two tries on each question, and every question explains itself after you answer.

Question 1
Which point is equidistant from all three vertices of a triangle?
Here's why: The circumcenter lies on every perpendicular bisector, and every point on a perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the segment's endpoints — so it's equidistant from all three vertices.
Question 2
Which point is equidistant from all three sides of a triangle?
Here's why: The incenter lies on every angle bisector, and points on an angle bisector are equidistant from the angle's sides.
Question 3
G is the centroid of ΔABC and lies on median AM̄. AG = 14. What is AM?
Here's why: The centroid splits a median 2 : 1 from the vertex, so GM is half of AG: 7. AM = 14 + 7 = 21. 28 doubles AG instead.
Question 4
The three altitudes of a triangle meet at the…
Here's why: Altitudes ↔ orthocenter. Medians make the centroid, angle bisectors the incenter, and perpendicular bisectors the circumcenter.
Question 5
Can segments of length 4, 7, and 12 form a triangle?
Here's why: The two shorter sides must add to more than the longest: 4 + 7 = 11, which is less than 12. The two short sides can't reach across.
Question 6
Two sides of a triangle measure 8 and 13. Which describes all possible lengths x of the third side?
Here's why: The third side is between the difference and the sum: 13 − 8 = 5 and 13 + 8 = 21. Both ends are strict, because a side of exactly 5 or 21 would flatten the triangle.
Question 7
In ΔABC, m∠A = 50°, m∠B = 60°, and m∠C = 70°. Which side is longest?
Here's why: The longest side is opposite the largest angle. ∠C is largest, and the side across from C is AB̄.
Question 8
You want to prove indirectly that ∠X is not a right angle. What is your first step?
Here's why: An indirect proof starts by assuming the opposite of the goal. The goal is "not right," so assume it is right, then find a contradiction.
Question 9
Two triangles each have sides 5 and 7. The angle between them is 40° in one and 65° in the other. Which is true?
Here's why: Hinge Theorem: with two pairs of congruent sides, the larger included angle opens the hinge wider and makes the longer third side.
Question 10
An exterior angle of a triangle measures 110°. Which can NOT be one of its remote interior angles?
Here's why: An exterior angle is greater than each remote interior angle, so neither can be 110° or more. 115° is impossible; the other values fit (for example, 60° + 50° = 110°).
