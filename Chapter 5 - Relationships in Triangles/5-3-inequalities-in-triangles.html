Let's Begin

Not just any three lengths make a triangle

So far in this chapter we've found special points inside triangles. Now we shift gears: today's lesson is about the rules — inequalities — that govern which side lengths and angle measures are even possible in a triangle in the first place.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Apply the Triangle Inequality Theorem to test whether three lengths can form a triangle
  • 2Apply the relationship between a triangle's largest angle and its longest side
  • 3Find the range of possible lengths for a triangle's third side

Core Theorem

The Triangle Inequality Theorem

The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must be greater than the length of the third side. If this fails for even one pair of sides, the three lengths cannot form a triangle at all.

WHY IT FAILS: TOO SHORT TO REACH

Worked Example

Testing whether a triangle can exist

Problem: Can side lengths 4, 7, and 12 form a triangle?

1
Check the largest pair first
4 + 7 = 11, and 11 is NOT greater than 12
2
Conclusion
Fails immediately — no triangle can be formed. (Shortcut: you only ever need to check whether the two smallest sides sum to more than the largest.)
3
Compare
4, 7, 10 would work: 4 + 7 = 11 > 10 ✓

Core Theorem

Bigger angle, bigger opposite side

Within one triangle, the largest angle is always opposite the longest side, and the smallest angle is always opposite the shortest side. The order of the angles from smallest to largest always matches the order of their opposite sides from shortest to longest.

ANGLE ORDER MATCHES OPPOSITE SIDE ORDER

Worked Example

Finding the range of the third side

Problem: Two sides of a triangle measure 6 and 15. What are the possible lengths, x, of the third side?

1
Lower bound
x must be greater than the difference of the two given sides: x > 15 − 6 = 9
2
Upper bound
x must be less than the sum of the two given sides: x < 15 + 6 = 21
3
Conclusion
9 < x < 21

Why This Matters

Checking feasibility before you build

Engineers and manufacturers use the Triangle Inequality Theorem to sanity-check specifications before cutting any material. If a design calls for a triangular brace with three lengths that violate the theorem, that brace is physically impossible to build — no amount of skill fixes an impossible geometry problem.

Real world: GPS and triangulation systems rely on the angle–side relationship constantly, using known angles from multiple towers to determine which distances are even mathematically plausible for a signal's location.
