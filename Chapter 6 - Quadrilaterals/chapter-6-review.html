Key Ideas · Lessons 6.1–6.3

Parallelograms

Lesson 6.1

The family tree

Every quadrilateral's angles sum to 360°. A parallelogram has both pairs of opposite sides parallel. A trapezoid has exactly one pair. Rectangles and rhombi are special parallelograms; a square is both.

Example: Every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.
Lesson 6.2

What a parallelogram guarantees

Opposite sides are congruent, opposite angles are congruent, consecutive angles are supplementary, and the diagonals bisect each other.

Example: In ▱ABCD, if m∠A = 70°, then m∠C = 70° and m∠B = 110°.
Diagonals bisect each other: AE = EC, BE = ED
Lesson 6.3

Five ways to prove it

A quadrilateral is a parallelogram if: both pairs of opposite sides are parallel, both pairs are congruent, both pairs of opposite angles are congruent, the diagonals bisect each other, or one pair of opposite sides is both parallel and congruent. One method is enough.

Example: Diagonals that bisect each other prove ▱ immediately.

Key Ideas · Lessons 6.4–6.5

Special quadrilaterals

Lesson 6.4

Rectangle, rhombus, square

A rectangle has four right angles and congruent diagonals. A rhombus has four congruent sides and perpendicular diagonals that bisect its angles. A square has every property of both.

Example: Rectangle diagonals 3x + 4 and 5x − 8 are equal, so x = 6 and each is 22.
Lesson 6.5

Trapezoids and kites

The midsegment of a trapezoid is parallel to the bases and equals their average: (b₁ + b₂)/2. An isosceles trapezoid has congruent legs, congruent base angles, and congruent diagonals. A kite has two pairs of consecutive congruent sides and perpendicular diagonals; it has no parallel sides.

Example: Bases 10 and 20 → midsegment 15.

Key Ideas · Lesson 6.6

Classifying with coordinates

Lesson 6.6

Slope for direction, distance for length

Equal slopes → parallel sides. Slopes with product −1 (or horizontal and vertical) → right angles. Equal distances → congruent sides. Use both tools to name the most specific shape.

Example: Four equal sides but no right angles → rhombus, not square.

Watch Out

The mistakes that cost the most points

The mistakeThe fix
Thinking consecutive angles of a parallelogram are equalOpposite angles are equal; consecutive angles add to 180°.
Assuming parallelogram diagonals are congruentOnly rectangles (and squares) have congruent diagonals. Every parallelogram's diagonals bisect each other.
"One pair parallel, the other pair congruent" proves a parallelogramNo — an isosceles trapezoid fits that description. The single-pair method needs the same pair parallel and congruent.
Calling every rhombus a squareA rhombus needs right angles to be a square.
Adding the trapezoid bases for the midsegmentAverage them: (b₁ + b₂)/2.
Stopping at "parallelogram" on a coordinate problemCheck sides and angles too — it may be a rectangle, rhombus, or square.

Practice Test

10 questions, every lesson mixed together

Just like a unit test, these jump between lessons. You get two tries on each question, and every question explains itself after you answer.

Question 1
A quadrilateral has angles of 85°, 95°, and 110°. What is the fourth angle?
Here's why: Quadrilateral angles sum to 360°: 85 + 95 + 110 = 290, and 360 − 290 = 70°. 250° uses 540°, the pentagon total.
Question 2
In parallelogram ABCD, m∠A = (3x + 10)° and m∠B = (5x − 6)°. What is m∠A?
Here's why: ∠A and ∠B are consecutive, so they're supplementary: 8x + 4 = 180, x = 22. m∠A = 3(22) + 10 = 76°. 104° is m∠B.
Question 3
The diagonals of parallelogram ABCD meet at E. AE = 2y + 3 and EC = 4y − 9. What is AC?
Here's why: Diagonals bisect each other, so AE = EC: 2y + 3 = 4y − 9, y = 6, AE = 15. AC is the whole diagonal: 15 + 15 = 30.
Question 4
Which information is NOT enough to prove a quadrilateral is a parallelogram?
Here's why: An isosceles trapezoid has one parallel pair and one congruent pair, and it isn't a parallelogram. The other three are valid tests.
Question 5
In rectangle ABCD, AC = 3x + 4 and BD = 5x − 8. What is BD?
Here's why: Rectangle diagonals are congruent: 3x + 4 = 5x − 8, so x = 6 and BD = 5(6) − 8 = 22.
Question 6
The diagonals of rhombus ABCD meet at E, and m∠AEB = (7x + 6)°. What is x?
Here's why: A rhombus's diagonals are perpendicular, so m∠AEB = 90°: 7x + 6 = 90, 7x = 84, x = 12.
Question 7
A trapezoid has bases 14 and x, and its midsegment is 19. What is x?
Here's why: Midsegment = (b₁ + b₂)/2: (14 + x)/2 = 19, so 14 + x = 38 and x = 24. 38 is the sum of the bases, not the missing base.
Question 8
In an isosceles trapezoid, one angle on the longer base measures 68°. What does each angle on the shorter base measure?
Here's why: Each leg makes a pair of consecutive angles between the parallel bases, which are supplementary: 180° − 68° = 112°. Both shorter-base angles are 112°, since base angles are congruent.
Question 9
Which statement is true of every kite?
Here's why: A kite's diagonals always cross at a right angle. Its congruent sides are consecutive, not opposite, and it has no parallel sides.
Question 10
Classify the quadrilateral with vertices A(0, 0), B(5, 0), C(8, 4), and D(3, 4).
Here's why: Each side has length 5 (for example BC = √(9 + 16) = 5), so it's a rhombus. AB has slope 0 and BC has slope 4/3, so there's no right angle — it is not a square.
