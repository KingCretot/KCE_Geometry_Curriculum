Welcome to Chapter 6: four-sided figures, sorted

You spent Chapter 5 inside triangles. Now we move to quadrilaterals — any closed shape with exactly four sides. Today's job is learning the family tree: which quadrilaterals are special cases of other quadrilaterals, and how to tell them apart.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

Core Concept

What makes something a quadrilateral

A quadrilateral is a closed plane figure with exactly four straight sides and four vertices. The interior angles of any quadrilateral always sum to 360° — you can prove this by drawing one diagonal, which splits the quadrilateral into two triangles (2 × 180° = 360°).

ANY QUADRILATERAL: 4 SIDES, ANGLES SUM TO 360°

Two Main Families

Parallelograms vs. trapezoids

1
Parallelogram
A quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel.
2
Trapezoid
A quadrilateral with exactly one pair of opposite sides parallel.
3
Neither
Some quadrilaterals (an irregular four-sided kite-like shape with no parallel sides at all) fit in neither family — they're just "quadrilateral."

See It

The quadrilateral hierarchy

Quadrilateral
Trapezoid
Parallelogram
Rectangle
Rhombus
Square

Reading this tree: every rectangle IS a parallelogram (it just has extra properties — right angles). Every square IS a rectangle AND a rhombus. Going down the tree adds requirements; going up the tree removes them. We'll build out this full tree over Lessons 6.2–6.4.

Worked Example

Classifying from a description

Problem: Quadrilateral ABCD has AB ∥ CD, but AD is not parallel to BC. Classify it.

1
Check the pairs
Only one pair of sides (AB and CD) is parallel.
2
Apply the definition
Exactly one pair of parallel sides matches the definition of a trapezoid.
3
Conclusion
ABCD is a trapezoid (and NOT a parallelogram, since parallelograms require both pairs parallel).

Why This Matters

Sorting shapes is how software "sees" geometry

Any computer vision or CAD system that identifies shapes in an image runs through exactly this kind of classification logic — checking side relationships and angle measures in order, from most general (quadrilateral) to most specific (square), to correctly label what it's looking at.

Real world: quilters, tile-setters, and furniture designers rely on this same hierarchy daily — knowing that a rhombus tile and a square tile share properties (all sides equal) helps them predict how patterns will repeat.
