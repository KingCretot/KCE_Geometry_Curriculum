The Big Idea Behind Half the Worksheet

"=" and "≅" are two different words for the same idea

= (equals) compares numbers — like lengths or degree measures. ≅ (congruent) compares shapes — like segments or angles themselves. The Definition of Congruent Segments is the rule that lets you translate between the two, and it works in both directions:

A
→
Measures equal → Segments congruent
If RS = TY (the numbers match), then RS̄ ≅ TȲ (the segments are congruent).
A
←
Segments congruent → Measures equal
If MN̄ ≅ RS̄, then MN = RS. Same rule, running the other way.
The tick marks mean "these two lengths are equal" — which is exactly what ≅ means for segments.

Fast way to remember it: = is for math class (numbers). ≅ is for art class (shapes). The definition is just the bridge between the two rooms.

Two "Obvious but Official" Rules

Postulates are facts so basic, we just agree to accept them

H
Line Postulate
Through any two points, there is exactly one line.
💡Think of two pushpins on a corkboard — there's only one way to stretch a string perfectly straight between them.
I
Segment Addition Postulate
If B is between A and C (written A-B-C), then AB + BC = AC. The two smaller pieces add up to the whole thing.
💡This is the ONE tool you'll use for every "find the missing length" algebra problem in this chapter — it turns a picture into an equation.
AB + BC = AC

The Three "Logic Moves"

Reflexive, Symmetric, Transitive — the moves every proof leans on

Worksheet item #1 says: "Segment congruence is an equivalence relation." That's just a fancy way of saying congruence follows all three of these rules — which is exactly why they're such a big deal in Chapter 2.

D
Reflexive Property
Anything is congruent (or equal) to itself. ∠A ≅ ∠A. Nothing fancy — a thing always equals itself.
💡Use this when a proof shares one side or one angle between two triangles/figures — "it's the same piece, so of course it matches itself."
B
Symmetric Property
You can flip the order. If m ⊥ n, then n ⊥ m. If A ≅ B, then B ≅ A.
💡Think of a handshake — if you shake my hand, I'm also shaking yours. The relationship goes both ways.
E
Transitive Property
If A connects to B, and B connects to C, then A connects to C. PQ̄≅RS̄ and RS̄≅TȲ → PQ̄≅TȲ.
💡Your friend's friend is (sort of) your friend — the middle term (RS) links the two ends together, then drops out of the final answer.

Items 9 & 10

Linear Pairs — angles that share a straight edge

Quick definition first, because you'll need it a lot: two angles are adjacent when they share a vertex AND a side, and don't overlap — like two slices of pizza sitting right next to each other. You'll see this word inside the definitions of linear pair (below), the angle bisector (later this chapter), and several worksheet items — so if a question says "adjacent," picture two angles touching along one edge, nothing fancier.

9
Answer: Angles that form a linear pair are supplementary.
A linear pair = two adjacent angles whose outer sides form a straight line.
10
Answer: If angles are adjacent and supplementary, they form a linear pair.
This is just #9 said backwards — same idea, reverse direction (just like items 4 and 6!).
a° + b° = 180° — because together they make the straight line.

Careful — common mix-up: a linear pair is ALWAYS supplementary, but supplementary angles aren't always a linear pair (they could be two separate angles anywhere that just happen to add to 180°). "Linear pair" is the stricter, more specific case.

Items 11–13

Three quick facts you need cold, no thinking required

Vertical Angles
Answer: congruent. Made by two crossing lines, sitting directly across from each other (like an "X"). They're always equal — no math needed to know that, just recognize the shape.
Supplementary Angles
Answer: sum of 180°. Memory trick: "S" for Supplementary and "S" for Straight (180° is a straight line).
Complementary Angles
Answer: sum of 90°. Memory trick: "C" for Complementary and "C" for Corner (90° is a square corner).
∠1 & ∠3 are vertical (congruent). ∠2 & ∠4 are vertical (congruent). ∠1 & ∠2 are a linear pair (supplementary).

Items 14–17 — the part everyone finds confusing

The "share a partner" theorems

These four all follow ONE pattern: if two angles are both related the same way to a third angle (or to each other), they end up congruent to each other. Here's how each one plays out:

14
Supplements of congruent angles are congruent.
If ∠1 supp. ∠2, and ∠3 supp. ∠4, and ∠2 ≅ ∠4, then ∠1 ≅ ∠3.
💡Two different angles paired with two EQUAL partners — the leftovers have to match too.
15
Supplements of the SAME angle are congruent.
If ∠1 and ∠2 are both supplementary to ∠3, then ∠1 ≅ ∠2.
💡This time both angles pair with the exact SAME third angle, not just an equal copy — slightly stricter, same result.
16
Complements of the same angle are congruent.
Exact same logic as #15, just with 90° pairs instead of 180° pairs.
17
Congruent AND supplementary angles are right angles.
If ∠1 ≅ ∠2 and ∠1 + ∠2 = 180°, then each one must be exactly 90°.
💡Two equal numbers that add to 180 can only be 90 and 90 — there's no other way to split it evenly.

The shortcut for all four: ask yourself "supplement (180) or complement (90)?" and "same angle/equal partner, or just congruent to each other?" — that combination tells you which of the four you're looking at.

Item 18

A midpoint always cuts a segment exactly in half

18
If M is the midpoint of AB, then AM = ½ AB.
That's also the same as saying AM = MB — both halves are equal, and each half is half the total.
Matching tick marks = matching lengths: AM = MB.

This is the algebra engine for the whole segment-problem section of your homework: whenever a diagram shows M as a midpoint, you can set the two halves EQUAL to each other and solve.

Items 19 & 20

An angle bisector does to angles what a midpoint does to segments

19
An angle bisector is a ray from the vertex, through the interior, forming two adjacent angles that are congruent to each other.
20
If BD bisects ∠ABC, then m∠ABD = m∠DBC = ½ · m∠ABC.
Ray BD splits ∠ABC into ∠ABD and ∠DBC — the matching gold tick marks show they're equal, adjacent halves.

Same pattern as the midpoint slide: midpoint → splits a SEGMENT in half. Angle bisector → splits an ANGLE in half. If you understand one, you understand the other.

Worked Example — Segment Algebra, Part 1

From your homework page: the AB and EF problems

M is the midpoint of AB. AM = 7.
1
Rule
M is the midpoint, so AM = MB.
2
Answer
MB = 7    AB = AM + MB = 7 + 7 = 14
M is the midpoint of EF. EM = 3x+2, MF = 5x−4.
1
Rule
Midpoint means the two halves are equal: EM = MF.
2
Set up the equation
3x + 2 = 5x − 4
3
Solve for x
2 + 4 = 5x − 3x  →  6 = 2x  →  x = 3
4
Plug x back in (Substitution Property!)
EM = 3(3)+2 = 11    MF = 5(3)−4 = 11    EF = 11+11 = 22

Faster way to check your work: after solving for x, both halves should come out to the SAME number (since it's a midpoint). If EM ≠ MF after you plug back in, go recheck your algebra — that's your built-in answer check.

Worked Example — Segment Algebra, Part 2

The CD and GH problems

M is the midpoint of CD. The whole segment CD = 16.
1
Rule
Midpoint splits the WHOLE in half: CM = MD = ½ CD.
2
Answer
CM = MD = 16 ÷ 2 = 8
M is the midpoint of GH. GM = 7x−9, MH = 3x+13.
1
Set equal
7x − 9 = 3x + 13
2
Solve
7x − 3x = 13 + 9  →  4x = 22  →  x = 5.5
3
Answer
GM = 7(5.5)−9 = 29.5    MH = 3(5.5)+13 = 29.5    GH = 59

Don't panic over decimals. x = 5.5 is a perfectly normal answer — geometry problems don't always give you whole numbers. Just carry the decimal through carefully.

Worked Example — Angle Algebra, Part 1

The straight-line and right-angle problems

118° and x° sit on a straight line — a linear pair.
1
Rule
Linear pair → supplementary → sum is 180°.
2
Solve
118 + x = 180  →  x = 62
The vertical ray is perpendicular to the line — each side of it is 90°. A third ray splits the right side into 34° and y°.
1
Left side
The small square marks a right angle, so x = 90.
2
Right side
The right side is ALSO 90° total (perpendicular works both directions), split into 34° and y°.
3
Solve
34 + y = 90  →  y = 56

Speed tip: whenever you see the small square (∟) symbol, that ENTIRE angle is 90° — even if it's been split by another ray into smaller pieces. Add the pieces, set equal to 90.

Worked Example — Angle Algebra, Part 2

Crossing lines: vertical angles with algebra

When two lines cross, you only ever get two different angle values — one pair of vertical angles share one measure, the other pair shares the supplement. Here's the general method, then the exact one from your worksheet.

One angle given (52°). x° and y° are its two supplementary neighbors.
1
Rule
Any angle right next to the given one is a LINEAR PAIR with it — supplementary.
2
Solve
x = 180 − 52 = 128     y = 180 − 52 = 128
3
Bonus fact
The angle directly across from 52° (its vertical angle) is ALSO 52° — you never need to calculate it, just copy it.

Same idea, now with algebra in the angle expressions

Same two crossing lines as above — the top angle and the bottom angle are directly opposite each other, so they're vertical angles.
1
Rule
Directly-opposite angles from two crossing lines are vertical angles — always equal. So set the two expressions equal to each other.
2
Set equal (vertical angles)
3x + 23 = 5x − 3
3
Solve
23 + 3 = 5x − 3x  →  26 = 2x  →  x = 13
4
Check
3(13)+23 = 62°     5(13)−3 = 62°  ✓ match

Once you have that one angle, you have all four: the left and right angles are each a linear pair with the 62° angles, so they're both 180−62 = 118°. Two rules — vertical angles are equal, linear pairs add to 180° — unlock every angle in the diagram from just one equation.

Mini Quiz

Show what you know — 8 quick questions

Question 1
If ∠A ≅ ∠A, that's an example of the...

Reflexive means "compared to itself" — one single angle, no partner involved, so Symmetric (flipping order) and Transitive (linking a middle term) don't apply here.

Question 2
Two angles that are both supplementary to the SAME angle must be...

This is the "supplements of the same angle" theorem from Slide 8 — both angles pair with the identical third angle, so the leftovers have to match each other.

Question 3
M is the midpoint of PQ. PM = 4x−1, MQ = 2x+9. What is x?

Midpoint → set the halves equal: 4x−1 = 2x+9  →  2x = 10  →  x = 5. Check: PM = 4(5)−1 = 19 and MQ = 2(5)+9 = 19 — they match, so x = 5 is confirmed.

Question 4
Two angles are congruent AND supplementary. Each one measures...

Two EQUAL numbers that add to 180 can only be 90 and 90 — there's no other way to split 180 evenly in half.

Question 5 — True or False
A linear pair and a pair of supplementary angles are always exactly the same thing.

Linear pair is the stricter case — it requires the angles to be adjacent AND for their outer sides to form a straight line. Two angles anywhere that just happen to add to 180° are supplementary but not necessarily a linear pair.

Question 6
Ray BD bisects ∠ABC. If m∠ABC = 68°, what is m∠ABD?

A bisector splits the WHOLE angle into two equal halves: m∠ABD = ½ · m∠ABC = ½ · 68° = 34°.

Question 7
"If RS = TY, then RS ≅ TY" uses which definition/property?

This is going from a NUMBER statement (=) to a SHAPE statement (≅) — that translation is exactly what the Definition of Congruent Segments does.

Question 8
∠A ≅ ∠B and ∠B ≅ ∠C. Which property lets you conclude ∠A ≅ ∠C?

∠B is the shared middle link connecting ∠A to ∠C, then drops out of the final statement — that hand-off through a middle term is exactly the Transitive Property.
