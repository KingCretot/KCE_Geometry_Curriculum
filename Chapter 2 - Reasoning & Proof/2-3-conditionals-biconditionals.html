Let's Begin

"If... then..." is the single most common sentence shape in geometry.

"If it's raining, then I'll bring an umbrella." You've used this exact sentence structure your whole life without a name for it. Today it gets one — and you'll start seeing it everywhere in this course, including in every postulate you learned in Chapter 1.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the hypothesis and conclusion of a conditional statement
  • 2Write the converse, inverse, and contrapositive of a conditional
  • 3Recognize a biconditional statement

Vocabulary

Conditional statement: the if-then sentence

A conditional statement has two parts: the hypothesis (the "if" part) and the conclusion (the "then" part).

IF (Hypothesis)
a shape is a square
THEN (Conclusion)
it has four right angles

💡 Even a sentence that doesn't literally use the words "if" and "then" can usually be rewritten into that form — that's often the very first step in a proof.

Rearranging the Pieces

Three ways to flip a conditional

Starting from a conditional, we can build three related statements by swapping and/or negating the hypothesis and conclusion:

FormHow it's builtExample
ConverseSwap hypothesis & conclusionIf four right angles, then square
InverseNegate both partsIf not a square, then not four right angles
ContrapositiveSwap AND negate bothIf not four right angles, then not a square

Worth remembering: a conditional and its contrapositive always share the same truth value. The converse and inverse can go either way — they don't automatically match the original.

A Special Case

Biconditional: when it works both ways

A biconditional statement combines a conditional and its converse using "if and only if" — and it's true only when BOTH directions are true.

A shape is a square
if and only if
it has four equal sides and four right angles
 

💡 Definitions in geometry are always biconditionals, even when they don't say "if and only if" out loud — that's part of why definitions can be used in both directions during a proof.

Worked Example

Building all four forms, step by step

Problem: Start with: "If an angle measures 90°, then it is a right angle."

1
Identify hypothesis & conclusion
Hypothesis: an angle measures 90°. Conclusion: it is a right angle.
2
Converse (swap)
If an angle is a right angle, then it measures 90°.
3
Inverse (negate)
If an angle does not measure 90°, then it is not a right angle.
4
Contrapositive (swap AND negate)
If an angle is not a right angle, then it does not measure 90°.

Why This Matters

Every postulate you already know is secretly a conditional

Go back and look at the Segment Addition Postulate from Chapter 1: "If B is between A and C, then AB + BC = AC." That's a conditional statement — hypothesis and conclusion, exactly like today. Recognizing this shape is what lets you use a postulate correctly in a proof.

Real world: warranties, insurance policies, and laws are almost always conditionals. "If the product fails within one year, then it will be replaced" — understanding hypothesis vs. conclusion is exactly what lets you know what a warranty actually promises.
