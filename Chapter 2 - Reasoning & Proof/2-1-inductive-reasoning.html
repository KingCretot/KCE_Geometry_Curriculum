Let's Begin — Welcome to Chapter 2!

You've been spotting patterns your whole life. Today we name it.

If every red light you've ever seen has turned green eventually, you expect the next one will too. That's not a guess out of nowhere — it's a real kind of reasoning, and geometry uses it constantly.

Chapter 1 was about the objects of geometry — points, lines, shapes. Chapter 2 is about the thinking behind geometry: how we notice patterns, make guesses, and eventually prove things for certain.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Use inductive reasoning to spot a pattern and predict what's next
  • 2Turn a pattern into a conjecture
  • 3Find a counterexample that breaks a conjecture

Vocabulary

Three words for "noticing a pattern"

1
Inductive Reasoning
Making a conclusion based on a pattern you've observed in specific examples. It's reasoning from examples toward a general rule.
2
Conjecture
An educated guess made using inductive reasoning. A conjecture is not proven yet — it's a strong hypothesis based on the pattern so far.
3
Counterexample
A single example that breaks a conjecture. Finding just one is enough to prove a conjecture false — no matter how many examples supported it before.

When Patterns Break

One counterexample is all it takes

Suppose someone makes this conjecture: "Every shape with 4 sides is a square." It might look true if you only ever draw squares. But geometry demands more than "looks true so far."

Counterexample: a rectangle that is 3 units wide and 6 units tall has 4 sides, but it is not a square. One shape is enough to prove the conjecture false.

💡 This is a big deal in math: a hundred supporting examples don't prove a conjecture true, but a single counterexample proves it false. That asymmetry is exactly why Chapter 2 exists — "looks true" and "is proven true" are very different things.

Worked Example

Building and testing a conjecture, step by step

Problem: Look at the sums: 1+3=4, 3+5=8, 5+7=12, 7+9=16. Make a conjecture about the sum of two consecutive odd numbers, then test it.

1
Find the pattern
Each sum (4, 8, 12, 16) is a multiple of 4.
2
State the conjecture
The sum of two consecutive odd numbers is always a multiple of 4.
3
Test it with a new example
Try 9+11 = 20. Is 20 a multiple of 4? Yes — 20 ÷ 4 = 5. The conjecture holds up again.
4
Remember the limit
Testing more examples makes us more confident — but it still isn't a proof. That comes later in this chapter.

Why This Matters

Inductive reasoning is where every discovery starts

Scientists, doctors, and mathematicians all start with inductive reasoning — noticing a pattern across many observations, then forming a conjecture. The difference between a hunch and real knowledge is what happens next: testing it, hunting for counterexamples, and eventually proving it.

Real world: a doctor who notices that a medication has helped 50 patients in a row is using inductive reasoning to form a conjecture — but a single patient it doesn't help (a counterexample) means the conjecture "this always works" has to be revised.
