Let's Begin — Last Lesson of Chapter 1!

Not every drawing needs to be exact — but some absolutely must be.

All chapter you've drawn points, lines, shapes, and solids by hand. Today we name the three different levels of care that go into a geometric drawing — and learn the ancient method for drawing something perfectly exact, using nothing but a compass and a straightedge.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Tell a sketch, a drawing, and a construction apart
  • 2Explain what tools a construction allows — and doesn't
  • 3Copy a segment using compass-and-straightedge construction

Vocabulary

Three levels of precision

SKETCH

Freehand

Quick, rough, no tools. Meant to capture the idea, not the exact measurements.

DRAWING

With Basic Tools

Ruler and protractor used for care and proportion — close, but still not mathematically perfect.

CONSTRUCTION

Compass & Straightedge Only

No rulers, no measuring, no numbers. Mathematically exact, every time.

💡 A construction's rule is strict on purpose: a straightedge draws lines but has no markings, and a compass only copies distances — it can't measure in inches or centimeters.

Why Such a Strict Rule?

Constructions come from a 2,000-year-old tradition

Around 300 BCE, the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote down geometry using only what a compass and straightedge could build. No rulers with inch marks existed yet in the way we use them — so "exact" had to mean "provably exact," not "measured closely enough."

Worked Example

Constructing a copy of a segment

Goal: Given segment AB, construct a new segment, CD, that is exactly the same length — using only compass and straightedge.

1
Draw a starting ray
Use the straightedge to draw a long ray starting at a new point, C.
2
Open the compass to match AB
Place the compass point on A and stretch it exactly to B — the compass now "remembers" that exact distance.
3
Transfer the distance
Without changing the compass width, place the point on C and swing an arc that crosses the ray.
4
Mark the new point
Where the arc crosses the ray is point D. Segment CD is now exactly as long as segment AB — guaranteed, with no measuring at all.

Seeing It Happen

The construction, drawn out

The gold dashed arc is the compass, still opened to the width of AB, swung from C. Where it crosses the ray gives point D.

Why This Matters

Constructions are proof you can hold in your hand

When you build something with only compass-and-straightedge rules, you're not just drawing — you're demonstrating that the result is logically guaranteed to be exact, not just "close enough by eye." That's the exact same standard proofs will hold you to starting in Chapter 2.

Historical note: for over 2,000 years, mathematicians tried to solve three famous "impossible" construction problems (like trisecting any angle) using only compass and straightedge. It was eventually proven impossible — a rare case of mathematicians proving something can't be done, not just that no one has done it yet.
