Let's Begin

In the real world, nothing is perfect. In geometry's world, everything is.

Your pencil line always has a little thickness. Your "straight" edge always wobbles a tiny bit. But in the world geometry imagines — the ideal geometry — lines have zero width and go on forever perfectly straight. We do our best to draw that perfect world on paper, even though paper can't quite keep up.

Last week you met point, line, and plane — the undefined terms. Today we add the first rules for how they behave: statements we simply accept as true, called postulates.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Explain what a postulate is, and how it's different from a theorem
  • 2State five key postulates about points, lines, and planes
  • 3Use a postulate to answer a question about a diagram

Vocabulary

Postulate vs. theorem — accepted vs. proved

1
Postulate (also called an Axiom)
A statement we accept as true without proof. It's a starting rule, not a conclusion. Every proof has to start somewhere — postulates are that starting point.
2
Theorem
A statement that has been proved — using postulates, definitions, and logic. You'll write your first proofs in Chapter 2, and every single one will lean on today's postulates.

💡 Think of postulates as the rules of a board game, and theorems as the winning strategies players figure out. Nobody proves the rules — you just agree to them and build everything else on top.

Postulates 1 & 2 — Points Determine Things

How many points does it take?

POSTULATE 1

Two Point Postulate

Through any two points, there is exactly one line.

POSTULATE 2

Three Point Postulate

Through any three non-collinear points, there is exactly one plane.

Postulates 3 & 4 — Intersections Drop a Dimension

What happens when things cross?

POSTULATE 3

Line Intersection Postulate

If two lines intersect, they intersect in exactly one point.

POSTULATE 4

Plane Intersection Postulate

If two planes intersect, they intersect in exactly one line.

💡 Notice the pattern: two lines (1-D) cross at a point (0-D). Two planes (2-D) cross in a line (1-D). Each intersection drops down one dimension.

Postulate 5 — Lines Living Inside Planes

If a line has two points in a plane...

POSTULATE 5

Flat Plane Postulate

If two points of a line lie in a plane, then the entire line lies in that plane.

💡 This is why a flat tabletop can hold a perfectly straight ruler lying on it end to end — once two points of the ruler are on the table, the whole ruler has to be.

Worked Example

Using postulates to answer a question

Problem: Lines j and k cross at point P. Someone claims lines j and k also cross at a second point, Q, farther down.

1
Which postulate applies?
The Line Intersection Postulate.
2
What does it say?
If two lines intersect, they intersect in exactly one point.
3
So is the claim possible?
No — two distinct lines can only cross once. If they crossed at both P and Q, they'd actually be the same line (by the Two Point Postulate).

Why This Matters

Postulates are the rulebook every proof will lean on

Starting in Chapter 2, you'll write formal proofs — and every single justification you write will point back to either a definition, a postulate, or a theorem already proved from postulates. You can't prove something from nothing; postulates are the "nothing" we all agree to start from.

Real-world version: chess players never prove why a knight moves in an L-shape — that's just a rule you accept. Every winning strategy (the "theorems" of chess) gets built on top of rules like that one.
