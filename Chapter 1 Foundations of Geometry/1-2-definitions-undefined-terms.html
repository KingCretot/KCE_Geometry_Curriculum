Let's Begin

Some things in math are too basic to define.

Last week you learned that every set has elements. Here's the twist: geometry's very first building blocks — point, line, and plane — are things we never fully define. We just agree what they mean and build everything else on top of them.

Think of it like the rules of a board game: someone has to say "this square is where you start" without explaining what a square is — everyone already knows. Point, line, and plane work the same way.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Describe a point, a line, and a plane, and draw each one
  • 2Name them correctly using geometry notation
  • 3Tell whether points are collinear or coplanar

Undefined Term 1 of 3

Point — an exact location, nothing more

PLANE · 2-D
•

Point

A point is an exact location. It has no size — no length, no width, no thickness. We just mark it with a dot so we have something to look at.

Written as: point P — always a capital letter

💡 The dot you draw isn't really the point — it's too big! A true point has zero size. The dot is just how we point at it (no pun intended).

Undefined Term 2 of 3

Line — a point stretched forever, in a straight path

POINT · 0-D
LINE · 1-D
PLANE · 2-D
↔

Line

A line is a straight path of points that goes on forever in both directions. It has length, but no width or thickness. Any two points determine exactly one line — there's only one straight path through both of them.

Written as: line AB, or with a double arrow over "AB", or a lowercase script letter like ℓ

💡 Those arrows on both ends aren't decoration — they're the notation telling you the line never stops. Compare that to 1.1: a line is really an infinite set of points.

Undefined Term 3 of 3

Plane — a flat surface with no edges

POINT · 0-D
LINE · 1-D
PLANE · 2-D
▯

Plane

A plane is a flat surface that goes on forever in every direction — think of an endless tabletop with no edges. It has length and width, but no thickness. Three points that aren't all on the same line determine exactly one plane.

Written as: plane MNQ, or a single script capital letter like plane 𝒜

💡 Why three points and not two? Two points only pin down a line — you could tilt a flat surface around that line in endless ways. A third point (off that line) locks the tilt in place.

Two New Words

Collinear & coplanar — things that share a home

The prefix "co-" means "together." Keep that in mind and both words explain themselves.

1
Collinear
Points that lie on the same line. ("Co" + "linear" = together on a line.)
2
Coplanar
Points that lie on the same plane. ("Co" + "planar" = together on a plane.)

A, B, and C are collinear (all on the line). D is not — it's off the line entirely.

Worked Example

Reading a diagram, step by step

Problem: Points X, Y, and Z sit on line m. Point W sits above the line, off to the side.

1
Are X, Y, Z collinear?
Yes — the problem says they're on line m together.
2
Is W collinear with X, Y, Z?
No — W is off the line, not on it.
3
Could X, Y, Z, and W still be coplanar?
Yes! A flat surface could hold the whole line and point W at the same time — being off the line doesn't mean being off the plane.
4
How would we name the line?
Line m, or line XY, or line YZ, or line XZ — any two of the three points work.

Why This Matters

Undefined terms are the foundation everything else stands on

Every shape, every proof, every formula in this course eventually traces back to point, line, and plane. That's exactly why geometry doesn't try to define them further — they're the starting alphabet, not something built from smaller pieces.

You already use these ideas every day

  • A pin on a map is a point — an exact location.
  • A tightrope, stretched perfectly straight, is a line.
  • A calm lake's surface is a plane.

Next week in Lesson 1.3, we'll meet postulates — statements about points, lines, and planes that we simply accept as true, no proof needed. Today's undefined terms are exactly what those postulates will be written about.
