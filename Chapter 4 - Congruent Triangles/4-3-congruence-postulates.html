Let's Begin

You don't need all six matches — just the right three

Checking all six corresponding parts every time would be exhausting. Good news: geometers discovered that certain combinations of just three matching parts are enough to guarantee two triangles are congruent. Today you learn the three most reliable shortcuts.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1State the SSS Theorem and the SAS and ASA Postulates
  • 2Identify which postulate applies to a given diagram
  • 3Recognize the included side/angle requirement in SAS and ASA

Shortcut 1 · Theorem

SSS — Side-Side-Side

SSS THEOREM

If all three sides of one triangle are congruent to all three sides of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.

MATCHING TICK MARKS = MATCHING SIDES

Three sides match — no angle information is even needed. Your textbook proves SSS as a theorem in Section 4.5; we use it now because it pairs naturally with SAS and ASA.

Shortcut 2 · Postulate

SAS — Side-Angle-Side

SAS POSTULATE

If two sides and the included angle of one triangle are congruent to two sides and the included angle of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.

"Included" matters: the angle must be the one formed directly between the two given sides — not just any angle in the triangle.

Shortcut 3 · Postulate

ASA — Angle-Side-Angle

ASA POSTULATE

If two angles and the included side of one triangle are congruent to two angles and the included side of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.

Same rule as SAS, flipped: here the included side must sit directly between the two given angles.

Worked Example

Identifying the right postulate

Problem: Two triangles share a common side. Each triangle also has one pair of congruent angles right at each end of that shared side. Which postulate proves them congruent?

1
Identify what's given
One side (shared), plus one angle at each endpoint of that side
2
Check the pattern
Angle, Side, Angle — and the side sits between the two angles
3
Conclusion
ASA Postulate applies

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Two triangles have all three pairs of corresponding sides marked congruent, and no angle information given.

Question 1 — Which postulate proves them congruent?

Here's why: All three pairs of sides match and nothing is said about angles, so SSS applies. Three fixed side lengths lock a triangle's shape, which is why triangular trusses keep bridges rigid. SAS would need an angle.

Question 2 — Two triangles have two congruent sides and a congruent angle, but the angle is NOT between the two given sides. Does SAS apply?

Here's why: No. SAS needs the angle to be between the two sides, like a hinge joining two boards. If the angle isn't between them, the third side can swing to more than one position.

Why This Matters

Three measurements instead of six, in the real world

A construction crew building matching roof trusses doesn't re-verify all six parts of each triangle — they cut to spec on three key measurements (like SAS: two beam lengths and the angle between them) and trust the congruence postulates to guarantee the rest matches.

Real world: engineers designing truss bridges rely on SSS, SAS, and ASA daily — verifying just a few critical measurements is far faster (and just as reliable) as checking every single part.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
SSS requires matching...
Here's why: SSS stands for Side-Side-Side, so all three pairs of sides must match. Three angles alone don't fix the size of a triangle.
Question 2
In SAS, the angle must be...
Here's why: In SAS, the angle has to be included, meaning it sits between the two given sides. That's the hinge that locks the third side in place.
Question 3
In ASA, the side must be...
Here's why: In ASA, the side has to be included, between the two given angles. The side connects the two angles' vertices, which fixes the triangle exactly.
Question 4
Two triangles share two congruent angles and the side between them. Which shortcut applies?
Here's why: Two angles with the side between them is ASA. SSS would need all three sides.
Question 5 — True or False
SSS, SAS, and ASA each require exactly three matching parts.
Here's why: True. SSS, SAS, and ASA each use exactly three matching parts. Three of the right parts are enough to guarantee all six match.
