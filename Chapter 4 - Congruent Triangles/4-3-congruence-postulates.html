Let's Begin

You don't need all six matches — just the right three

Checking all six corresponding parts every time would be exhausting. Good news: geometers discovered that certain combinations of just three matching parts are enough to guarantee two triangles are congruent. Today you learn the three most reliable shortcuts.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1State the SSS, SAS, and ASA Postulates
  • 2Identify which postulate applies to a given diagram
  • 3Recognize the included side/angle requirement in SAS and ASA

Postulate 1

SSS — Side-Side-Side

SSS POSTULATE

If all three sides of one triangle are congruent to all three sides of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.

MATCHING TICK MARKS = MATCHING SIDES

Three sides match, in any three-sided figure — no angle information is even needed.

Postulate 2

SAS — Side-Angle-Side

SAS POSTULATE

If two sides and the included angle of one triangle are congruent to two sides and the included angle of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.

"Included" matters: the angle must be the one formed directly between the two given sides — not just any angle in the triangle.

Postulate 3

ASA — Angle-Side-Angle

ASA POSTULATE

If two angles and the included side of one triangle are congruent to two angles and the included side of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.

Same rule as SAS, flipped: here the included side must sit directly between the two given angles.

Worked Example

Identifying the right postulate

Problem: Two triangles share a common side. Each triangle also has one pair of congruent angles right at each end of that shared side. Which postulate proves them congruent?

1
Identify what's given
One side (shared), plus one angle at each endpoint of that side
2
Check the pattern
Angle, Side, Angle — and the side sits between the two angles
3
Conclusion
ASA Postulate applies

Why This Matters

Three measurements instead of six, in the real world

A construction crew building matching roof trusses doesn't re-verify all six parts of each triangle — they cut to spec on three key measurements (like SAS: two beam lengths and the angle between them) and trust the congruence postulates to guarantee the rest matches.

Real world: engineers designing truss bridges rely on SSS, SAS, and ASA daily — verifying just a few critical measurements is far faster (and just as reliable) as checking every single part.
