Let's Begin

Classifying triangles with nothing but coordinates

Back in Chapter 3, you learned the Distance, Midpoint, and Slope Formulas. Today we put them to work identifying triangle types — isosceles, right, or neither — using only the (x, y) coordinates of the three vertices. No protractor, no ruler, just algebra.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Use the Distance Formula to determine if a triangle is isosceles
  • 2Use slope to determine if a triangle is a right triangle
  • 3Classify a triangle fully using coordinate methods

Recall from Chapter 3

Your two key tools

Distance Formula
d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
Slope Formula
m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)

Distance tells us side lengths (isosceles check). Slope tells us whether two sides are perpendicular (right-triangle check) — recall from Lesson 3.4 that perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals of each other.

Worked Example

Testing for isosceles using distance

Problem: Triangle has vertices A(0, 0), B(4, 0), C(2, 3). Is it isosceles?

1
Find AC
√[(2−0)² + (3−0)²] = √13
2
Find BC
√[(2−4)² + (3−0)²] = √13
3
Conclusion
AC = BC = √13, so ΔABC is isosceles

Worked Example

Testing for a right triangle using slope

SAME TRIANGLE, CHECKING FOR A RIGHT ANGLE
1
Slope of AC
(3−0)/(2−0) = 3/2
2
Slope of BC
(3−0)/(2−4) = −3/2
3
Conclusion
3/2 and −3/2 are not negative reciprocals (that would be 3/2 and −2/3), so ∠C is not a right angle — this triangle is isosceles, but not right

Worked Example

A triangle that IS a right triangle

Problem: Triangle has vertices D(0, 0), E(4, 0), F(0, 3). Is it a right triangle?

1
Slope of DE
(0−0)/(4−0) = 0 (horizontal)
2
Slope of DF
(3−0)/(0−0) = undefined (vertical)
3
Conclusion
A horizontal side and a vertical side meet at a perfect right angle — ΔDEF is a right triangle, with the right angle at D

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Triangle has vertices P(1, 1), Q(5, 1), R(3, 5).

Question 1 — Using the Distance Formula, PR = √20 and QR = √20. What does this tell you?

Here's why: PR = QR, and two equal sides make a triangle isosceles. To know if it's a right triangle, you'd have to check slopes or the Pythagorean Theorem.

Question 2 — Side PQ is horizontal (slope 0). To check for a right angle at P, what would you need to check?

Here's why: PQ is horizontal, so a right angle at P would need PR to be vertical, with an undefined slope. Horizontal and vertical lines always meet at 90°. Comparing lengths tells you about isosceles, not about right angles.

Why This Matters

Classifying shapes from data alone

A GIS analyst mapping property boundaries, or a video game engine testing whether a collision shape is a right triangle, never gets to physically measure anything — they only have coordinates. Distance and slope are exactly the tools that turn raw (x, y) data into real geometric conclusions.

Real world: architecture and CAD software constantly classify shapes this way — every wall, roof pitch, and triangular truss in a digital blueprint is checked using distance and slope, exactly as you did today.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
To check if a triangle is isosceles using coordinates, use the...
Here's why: Isosceles means two equal sides, so you need side lengths, and that's what the Distance Formula gives you. Slopes tell you about angles, not lengths.
Question 2
Two sides of a triangle have slopes 2 and −1/2. What does this tell you?
Here's why: 2 × (−1/2) = −1, so the slopes are negative reciprocals and the two sides are perpendicular. That means the triangle has a right angle.
Question 3
Triangle vertices A(0,0), B(6,0), C(3,4). AC = BC = 5. What type of triangle is this (at minimum)?
Here's why: AC = BC = 5, so two sides are equal and the triangle is at least isosceles. A scalene triangle has all three sides different.
Question 4
One side of a triangle is horizontal and another is vertical. What can you conclude?
Here's why: Horizontal and vertical lines are always perpendicular, like the edges of a sheet of graph paper. Where those two sides meet, the triangle has a right angle.
Question 5 — True or False
You can fully classify a triangle's type using only its vertex coordinates, without measuring anything by hand.
Here's why: True. The Distance Formula tells you the side lengths (scalene, isosceles, or equilateral), and slopes tell you whether there's a right angle. You can classify the whole triangle from coordinates alone.
